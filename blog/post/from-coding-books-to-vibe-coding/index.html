From Coding Books to Vibe Coding: Why I Believe We’re Witnessing a Once-in-a-Generation Shift

Back in 2019, while I was living in New Zealand, I ordered my first coding books. I had always wanted to build software, but I never went to school for computer science. At the time, becoming a software developer felt like something that would take years of study.

Instead, I found another path.

I began building systems with AppSheet, Google Sheets, APIs, automations, and eventually AI. Those tools taught me something more important than syntax. They taught me how data connects, how software solves problems, and how different systems communicate with one another.

I wrote more about that journey in Spiderweb of Data, where I describe how I started seeing software less as a collection of individual files and more as a web of interconnected systems.

Since then, AI has accelerated faster than I ever imagined.

The term “vibe coding” was coined by Andrej Karpathy in early 2025 to describe building software by collaborating with AI through natural language instead of writing every line of code yourself. What sounded experimental just a short time ago has quickly become a rapidly growing industry.

One recent industry estimate values the vibe coding market at approximately $4.7 billion, with annual growth approaching 38%. Even more interesting, it estimates that about 63% of active users are non-developers. Whatever the exact boundaries of this emerging market, the direction is clear: software creation is becoming accessible to people who never followed the traditional computer science path.

The broader economic trends point in the same direction. The Nasdaq Economic Institute recently analyzed U.S. Census Bureau business formation data and found that applications for one-person businesses have increased by more than 20% since early 2025. Its economists believe AI is helping entrepreneurs build businesses that might once have required employees, outside developers, or significant startup capital.

Businesses are already reporting measurable results. Intuit’s 2026 AI Impact Report, based on surveys of more than 34,000 small and midsize business owners and anonymized data from 5.3 million QuickBooks businesses, found that among U.S. respondents:

  • 77% now use AI regularly.
  • 78% report improved productivity.
  • 43% say AI has increased their revenue.
  • Only 2% report that AI has reduced their revenue.

What excites me isn’t just the market size.

It’s the opportunity.

For the first time, people with ideas but without formal computer science degrees can build software that would previously have required an entire development team. AI dramatically lowers the barrier to entry, but it doesn’t eliminate the need to understand systems.

I’ve experienced that firsthand.

On any given day, I might be connecting Stripe payments, configuring environment variables in Vercel, deploying Supabase Edge Functions, managing authentication, writing database migrations, securing APIs, and debugging webhooks. AI can generate much of the code, but it can’t automatically design a reliable architecture or understand the business decisions behind every connection.

In many ways, AI is becoming the world’s best programming assistant. But you are still responsible for designing the system it builds.

Looking back, I don’t think buying those coding books in 2019 was really about learning HTML or JavaScript.

It was about learning how to think.

That mindset carried me through AppSheet, APIs, automation, AI, and now building software with tools that simply didn’t exist a few years ago.

Some people have asked why I’ve dedicated so much time to learning these systems, even before they became mainstream. This is why.

I genuinely believe we’re watching one of the biggest shifts in software development since the internet became mainstream. Some people are finding incredible opportunities through social media or e-commerce. I chose to spend my time learning AI-assisted software development because I believe the ability to transform ideas into working systems will be one of the defining skills of the next decade.

I’m still early in that journey. It hasn’t been the most profitable path yet.

But when I step back and look at where this technology is heading, I become even more convinced that I’m learning the right skills at exactly the right time.
