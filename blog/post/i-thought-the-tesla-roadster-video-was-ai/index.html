I Thought the Tesla Roadster Video Was AI

I saw a video on Instagram of the new Tesla Roadster and honestly did not believe it. I thought it had to be AI. Or “SI.” So I went to YouTube, typed in Tesla, and watched the event. I could not believe what I was seeing.

But the first thing I think about when I see something like this is how exciting it would be to be on that stage as one of the engineers who built it. To know what went into making it work, and then finally get to show everyone.

I get so excited to release a new tool or site once I build it. Especially something that runs more advanced scripts, analyzes incoming data, and actually responds to what is happening. Seeing that kind of work come together in a vehicle makes me want to understand everything behind it. I would love to be part of a process like this.

Watch the full Roadster event on YouTube.

How does it even do that?

The part where the car goes upside down is what really got me. Tesla describes a fan and ground-sealing system that pulls air out from underneath the vehicle. That lowers the pressure beneath it and creates downforce, even when the car is sitting still. During the event, the team claimed the suction system could pull up to two Gs.

So it does not have to be moving fast to get that grip. The fans can help pull it toward the surface at low speed. At higher speeds, Tesla says passages in the suction system open to increase airflow underneath the car. The rear wing also adjusts automatically based on what the car calculates it needs going around a track.

That is the part I keep thinking about from a developer perspective. Taking in information, figuring out what needs to happen, and making the right adjustment at the right time. Except here those adjustments are changing how an entire vehicle moves.

The compressed air side of it

Then there are the cold-gas thrusters. They release stored, highly pressurized gas through nozzles to produce thrust. Tesla says electric actuators make very small adjustments within fractions of a second to help stabilize the vehicle in flight.

At the event, the team described the demonstration car as having two pressure vessels with a combined volume of 600 liters, holding about 250 kilograms of air, connected to ten nozzles. These are carbon fiber overwrapped pressure vessels, the kind of hardware that makes the SpaceX connection a little easier to understand.

Musk said the car could recharge its own air tank. That immediately makes me curious about the compressor: how much energy it takes, how long it takes, and how you fit that whole system into the car. The presentation did not establish a refill time or battery energy cost, so those are details I still want to learn more about.

What versions are actually planned?

Tesla currently lists three configurations on its Roadster page. The differences matter, because the car doing aerial maneuvers is not the same configuration as the basic road version.

  • Performance: Two seats, active aerodynamics, and the multi-mode suction system. Tesla describes this as road ready and available by reservation.
  • Thrust: A limited-release, two-seat configuration with rear cold-gas thrusters. Tesla describes it as race ready and claims 0–60 mph in under one second.
  • Rocket: An invitation-only, single-seat configuration with thrusters around the perimeter for forward, upward, and rotational movement. Tesla explicitly says this version is not approved for public road use.

At the October 15, 2026 event, Musk gave 2028 as the production target. That is still a target. What we are watching right now is a demonstration, and there is more work between showing what a vehicle can do and delivering it to customers.

Wanting to be part of building it

I know building a website or a software tool is a different kind of project. But I recognize that excitement of having an idea, working through all the things that do not work, and finally seeing it do what you pictured.

When I watch this, I think about the people who had to figure it out. The engineers writing the software, designing the hardware, testing it, and getting all those systems to work together. What would it feel like to watch that demonstration after spending so much time building it?

I would love to experience that. To contribute something, understand my part in the process, and then get to stand there with the people who made it happen.

For now, it makes me want to keep learning and building. I already get excited about the things I can create from my computer. Seeing something like this just makes me think about how much more there is to learn.

It’s such a wild time to be alive.
